# Positioning

Before researching deeply or drafting, define the transformation the book gives a
specific kind of reader. A weak premise is topic-led ("a book about leadership").
A strong premise is reader-and-outcome-led.

## The one-sentence test

> For **[specific reader]**, this book helps them **[achieve outcome]** by
> **[distinct approach]**.

Example:

> For homeowners in older houses, this book helps them prioritize repairs and
> avoid expensive mistakes by teaching a risk-based inspection system.

The sentence must answer five questions:

| Question | What you must decide |
|---|---|
| Who is it for? | A concrete primary reader — not "everyone interested in…" |
| What problem do they have? | A costly, frustrating, confusing, urgent, or aspirational problem |
| What outcome do they want? | A visible change in skill, knowledge, behavior, or decision quality |
| What is your unique angle? | Your framework, access, experience, evidence, synthesis, or voice |
| Why does this need to be a book? | It needs sustained explanation, a system, stories, examples, or reference value |

If any answer is vague, positioning is not done.

## The six book types

Most nonfiction blends modes, but **one should dominate**.

- **Prescriptive** — teaches what to do. Business, self-help, health, finance,
  how-to.
- **Explanatory** — makes a complex subject understandable. Science, history,
  policy, technology.
- **Narrative** — tells a true story with scenes, people, chronology, stakes.
- **Argument-driven** — advances and supports a thesis. Politics, culture,
  economics, ideas.
- **Memoir / reported memoir** — connects lived experience with a broader
  insight.
- **Reference / field guide** — organized for repeated consultation.

**Mode-mixing failures.** A history book with a self-help structure feels
shallow. A practical book that spends too long narrating background frustrates
readers who came for tools. Decide which mode governs, then let the others serve
it.

## Validate before writing

Quality includes relevance. A well-written book with no defined audience, market
position, or differentiation is hard to publish and harder to sell.

### Study comparable books

Find 10–20 books overlapping in audience, category, promise, or tone — books
competing for the same reader's attention, not only the same topic. For each,
capture: title, subtitle, publication date, publisher, approximate length; the
cover / sales-page promise; the table of contents and structure; the central
framework or thesis; recurring praise and recurring complaints in reviews; what
the book omits, oversimplifies, assumes, or leaves unresolved; why your version
would be meaningfully different.

The goal is not "no one has written this". It is:

> Existing books help readers do X. This book helps **this particular reader**
> achieve Y using Z — which existing books do not combine or explain well.

### Interview intended readers

Interview 10–20 members of the audience. Do **not** pitch the book first. Ask
about current behavior and language:

- What is difficult about this problem right now?
- What have you tried?
- What advice have you found unhelpful or unrealistic?
- What would success look like in six or twelve months?
- What terms do you use to describe the problem?
- Which books, creators, courses, or experts do you already trust?
- What would make you skeptical of a book on this subject?

This yields the reader's actual vocabulary, objections, constraints, examples,
and desired outcomes — more valuable than generic feedback.

### Premortem the premise

Recommended: run [`unknown-discovery`](../../unknown-discovery/SKILL.md) — a
premortem ("this book failed; why?"), an assumption register for the thesis with
a disconfirming signal per assumption, and the "what would make a reader
skeptical" list treated as hypotheses to test.

## The positioning brief

One to two pages, the decision document for the whole project. Ten fields:
working title and subtitle; one-sentence promise; target reader; reader's
starting and desired end state; core problem and stakes; central thesis or
framework; three to five comparable titles; author credibility; provisional table
of contents; **what the book explicitly will not cover**.

The last field matters most. High-quality books are as much about disciplined
exclusion as inclusion. A book that will not say what it excludes has not decided
what it is.
