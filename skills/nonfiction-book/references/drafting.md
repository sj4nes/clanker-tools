# Drafting

The first draft has one job: produce material you can revise. Do not demand
polished prose and comprehensive fact-checking at the same moment — different
cognitive tasks. Voice stays the author's; the agent supports, it does not
ghostwrite.

## Chapter-by-chapter sequence

1. Review the chapter brief.
2. Gather only the sources and ledger rows that chapter needs. Use
   [`csplit`](../../csplit/SKILL.md) to keep per-chapter working files so the
   author is not drafting with the whole library open.
3. Draft an opening that establishes a question, tension, scene, surprise, or
   reader problem.
4. State the main idea early.
5. Develop it with a deliberate mix of evidence, explanation, examples, and
   application.
6. Address a credible objection or limitation.
7. End with an implication, action, or bridge to the next chapter.

**Practical chapter pattern:**
hook → problem → principle → evidence → example → method → pitfalls → reader
action → transition.

**Narrative chapter pattern:**
scene → question → context → complication → revelation → consequence → forward
pull.

Flag unresolved research and move on. Do not stall a chapter on one missing fact;
add it to the unresolved-research list with the ledger row id.

## Write at the right altitude

Nonfiction fails when it stays too abstract or drowns in detail. Move
deliberately among three levels:

- **Zoom out** — the big idea, stakes, historical context, framework.
- **Middle distance** — explanation, mechanism, comparison, evidence.
- **Zoom in** — a person, decision, scene, number, quote, object, or moment.

Worked example:

- *Abstract:* "Poor onboarding increases early turnover."
- *Explanation:* "New employees often lack role clarity, feedback loops, and
  social integration."
- *Concrete:* "On her third day, Maya had a laptop but no manager meeting, no
  project brief, and no idea which team owned the customer data she was asked to
  analyze."

Concrete detail makes the reader care; analysis tells them why it matters. A
passage that has one without the other is a flag.

## Make prose easy to trust

Trustworthy prose is specific rather than inflated; clear about who, what, when,
and under what conditions; careful with causal claims; candid about exceptions
and uncertainty; free of unnecessary jargon; written with verbs that show who is
acting.

Prefer:

> In a 2024 survey of 1,200 small-business owners, respondents who used weekly
> cash-flow forecasts reported fewer late payments.

Over:

> Cash-flow forecasting is a transformational practice that empowers businesses
> to unlock resilience.

The first can be checked; the second only sounds persuasive.

## The eight quality traps

Flag these in any draft:

1. **Research dumping** — facts without argument, story, or relevance.
2. **Framework inflation** — naming every observation a "method", "system", or
   "secret".
3. **Unsupported certainty** — treating correlation, limited evidence, or
   personal experience as proof.
4. **Generic examples** — examples that could apply to anyone and so persuade no
   one.
5. **Premature advice** — telling readers what to do before helping them
   understand the problem.
6. **Repetition disguised as emphasis** — restating a claim with no added
   evidence, nuance, or application.
7. **Unexamined bias** — ignoring incentives, cultural context, selection
   effects, counterexamples.
8. **Overreliance on quotations** — quoted material should support the author's
   voice, not replace it.

## If asked to draft prose

Produce it clearly marked as a draft to be rewritten in the author's voice.
Record which claim-ledger rows it leans on. Do not present machine-drafted prose
as the author's own.
