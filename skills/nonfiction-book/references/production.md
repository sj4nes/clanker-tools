# Manuscript preparation and production

Publishing paths differ, but the manuscript must be complete, organized, and
production-ready either way. This skill's spine ends at a production-ready
manuscript and an organized project folder. Marketing, pricing, cover-design
execution, interior design, and distribution are a separate discipline and out of
scope — the notes below are orientation, not a workflow.

## Traditional publishing

Many nonfiction books are sold on **proposal** rather than a finished manuscript,
particularly when the author has recognized expertise, a strong platform, or a
timely concept. A proposal functions like a business case. It commonly includes:

- Title and subtitle.
- Overview or elevator pitch.
- Target audience and reader need.
- Author biography and credentials.
- Comparable titles and differentiation.
- Marketing and publicity plan.
- Detailed annotated table of contents.
- Sample chapters.
- Estimated word count, schedule, and any special production needs.

Do **not** fabricate an audience, endorsements, platform, or market data.
Agents and publishers expect a credible case, not inflated claims. The positioning
brief, comps table, and fat outline feed most of the proposal directly.

## Self-publishing

Self-publishing gives control and makes the author responsible for the full
production chain: developmental editing; copyediting; proofreading; cover design
appropriate to genre and audience; interior design and ebook formatting; ISBN and
metadata decisions; accessibility elements such as descriptive alt text;
distribution setup, launch plan, and review process.

Regardless of route, the book is a product: its title, subtitle, cover,
description, category, pricing, formats, metadata, and opening pages all shape
whether the right reader finds and trusts it.

## Manuscript hygiene

Publisher-facing guidance expects a complete manuscript: title page, table of
contents, full text, and any needed back matter. Artwork, captions, tables, and
accessibility descriptions need deliberate handling **before** copyediting.
Editorial standards such as the Chicago Manual of Style provide established
conventions for manuscript preparation, editing, proofreading, and notes or
author-date citation systems — pick one in the style sheet and hold to it.

## The project folder

Keep one folder with:

- Manuscript master file.
- Source library and citation database.
- Permissions log.
- Image / table inventory (with alt text).
- Interview recordings and transcripts, with consent details.
- The claim ledger.
- The style sheet.
- The feedback tracker.
- Version history.
- Marketing and proposal materials (if traditional route).

## The realistic end-to-end workflow

1. Define the reader and promise — the one-sentence positioning statement.
2. Validate demand and differentiation — comps + reader interviews.
3. Write the positioning brief — audience, angle, scope, exclusions.
4. Discovery research — map the field, debates, vocabulary, authorities, evidence
   gaps, potential stories.
5. Create the claim ledger and source system — every important assertion
   traceable.
6. Build the fat outline — each chapter a reader question, claim, evidence plan,
   example, takeaway.
7. Draft a representative chapter — test tone, structure, evidence density,
   reader value before writing all chapters.
8. Draft the full manuscript — maintain momentum; flag unresolved research.
9. Developmental revision — premise, structure, ordering, omissions, repetition,
   pacing.
10. Fact-check and expert review — tighten claims and citations.
11. Beta readers — test comprehension and usefulness with real target readers.
12. Line edit, copyedit, proofread — macro to micro.
13. Prepare for the publishing route — proposal, or production files.
14. Plan discoverability — align title, subtitle, cover, metadata, description
    with the reader promise. *(Out of scope for this skill.)*
